Hawaiian Honeycreeper Extinction — Ribbon Phylogeny

A Sankey-style family tree of 61 honeycreeper lineages. Each ribbon is one species: its width is how many birds there were, and it tapers to a point when the species is lost.

Read left to right. The honeycreepers descend from one finch-like founder that reached Hawaiʻi roughly 6–7 million years ago (left, deep time). Every fork is a branch of the DNA family tree; every ribbon leaving a fork is a lineage, and a clade's ribbon is as thick as all of its members together. Ribbons start thin, widen as the group radiates, and after the axis break — where the last 1,000 years are shown in calendar years — they follow documented population and narrow to a point at the year of extinction. Survivors fly off the right edge; extinct species lie on their backs where their ribbon ends. Within every clade the survivors sit on top, so the living species gather at the top of the chart and the lost ones fill the rest. Hover a ribbon (or a clade bar) for its record.

Era:

Methodology

What the picture is. A phylogenetic tree drawn as an alluvial (Sankey-style) diagram. Every fork is a node bar and the ribbons leaving it are the lineages that split off there. At any position along the radiation a clade's ribbon is exactly as thick as the sum of its members, so flow is conserved across the tree — but unlike a textbook Sankey, width is not conserved through time: a lineage can grow, shrink and finally end.

Topology. Branching order comes from the maximum-likelihood trees of Campana et al. (2026, PNAS; Figshare doi:10.25573/data.28914329), which include DNA from museum skins and subfossil bones. Forks resting on DNA are drawn as such; lineages with no usable DNA are placed from taxonomy (Olson & James 1991; Pratt 2014) and their ribbons are hatched. The stem is the ≈7.2 mya split from the Asian rosefinches (Carpodacus; Lerner et al. 2011).

Dates. The published trees are not time-calibrated, so fork positions are approximate and partly schematic: for the best-sampled clades a fork's depth below the crown was scaled to the 5.7 mya crown age of Lerner et al. (2011) in three trees (autosomal, mitochondrial, hybridization-capture) and rounded onto a 0.3-million-year lattice so forks fall into columns; poorly sampled or DNA-less forks were placed on the same lattice below their parent, and the three trees disagree by up to about a million years. Read fork positions as “about when, and in what order”, not as dated events. The deep-time axis is piecewise-linear (room for the radiation, less for quiet stretches) and is broken once, between 0.5 mya and 1000 CE; the last 1,000 years run in calendar years, stretched toward the present.

Layout. Two arrangements are blended. One is the compact alluvial stack (ribbons flush against each node bar — the trunk). The other gives every species its own row with generous spacing, survivors first inside each clade. The ribbons follow a smooth blend of the two, so the trunk fans out into 61 rows along curved paths that never cross and never overlap (a convex blend of two non-overlapping arrangements; verified numerically by poc/_render/check_model.js). The fan is complete before the axis break.

Width = population. A log scale: 2.5 px at 50 birds up to 21.5 px at two million. Where counts exist the ribbon follows them (interpolated on a log scale between anchor years; dots mark real survey counts). Every other species gets an assumed tier (abundant / common / rare / critical), flagged in its tooltip and in the data table. Population before roughly 1900 is an assumed baseline, never data. Lineages are drawn born at 60% of their tier width and widening as the radiation fans out — that widening is modeled, not measured.

Taper. An extinct ribbon keeps its width, then narrows over its last ≈100 px to a point at the year of extinction (width × (1 − s1.8)). This is a visual convention for decline, not a population history: counts do not exist for most extinct species. Subfossil species with no extinction date are spread across the Polynesian-era window (1000–1778 CE); that spread is arbitrary and is called out in the tooltip.

Driver dots. The small colored dot beside each extinct species' name is a rule-of-thumb guess at the main driver of loss — avian malaria for losses after about 1890, introduced predators or habitat loss for a few species with a documented cause, “earlier losses” for the rest, “unknown” for undated subfossils. It is a heuristic, not a per-species finding.

Birds. Each silhouette is generated from the species' measurements in AVOTREX and AVONET (assets/hc_glyphs.js; try the sampler): body length from wing length, body depth and head size from mass, tail and legs from tail and tarsus length, bill length and thickness from the bill measurements. For the 21 subfossil species AVOTREX imputes every measurement from skeletal remains, and most extinct body masses are imputed too, so those silhouettes are estimates. Bill curvature (sickle, decurved, hooked, conical) and the short lower mandible of the nukupuʻu and ʻākiapōlāʻau are qualitative, taken from the species accounts. Extinct birds lie belly-up with their feet in the air, tail pointing back at the ribbon that ended, so a curved bill curves up; survivors fly. All birds share one scale per pose, so a big koa-finch is drawn bigger than an ʻanianiau.

Data difficulties (flagged, not hidden)

  • Extinction years conflict across sources. Maui ʻākepa (1983 vs 1988), ʻōʻū (1989 vs 1998), ʻula-ʻai-hāwane (1892 vs 1937), koa-finches (1891/1896 vs pre-1795, James & Prince 2008). Each is called out in its tooltip ⚠ data difficulty.
  • AVONET still lists 5 extinct species as “Extant”. Maui and Kauaʻi nukupuʻu, Maui ʻākepa, Oʻahu ʻalauahio, ʻōʻū — a taxonomic snapshot, not conservation status. Re-keyed here as extinct. A red ring on a label marks the six honeycreepers USFWS declared extinct in 2023.
  • Subfossil-only lineages have no DNA. They are hatched so inference reads apart from molecular evidence; the home island of several is unresolved and flagged as such.
  • ʻAkikiki is drawn as living with ≈1 bird: the last wild individual was seen in August 2025, and about 40 survive in captivity.
  • Two genus keys changed in Campana 2026. ʻiʻiwi = Vestiaria_coccinea (AVONET Drepanis_coccinea); ʻalawī = Loxops_mana (AVONET Manucerthia_mana). Both keys are recorded in the CSV.
